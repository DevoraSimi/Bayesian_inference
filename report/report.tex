\begin{abstract}
This project compares models for next-word prediction: given the text so far, suggest the most likely next word. The models take different
approaches: an $n$-gram model, which predicts from counts of the last few words; Hidden Markov Models (HMMs), which capture the context in hidden states, trained with Baum-Welch and with Variational Bayes; and a Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) model, a type of recurrent neural network (RNN). Each is tested with different settings, such as the number of hidden states or the network size. The models are trained on the Sherlock Holmes stories. The LSTM gives the best predictions, while the $n$-gram model is the simplest and the most interpretable.
\end{abstract}

\section{Problem Statement}
% ============================================================

The task is {word-level next-word prediction}: given the tokens
$w_1, \dots, w_{t-1}$ of a paragraph, predict a distribution over the
next token $w_t$ from a fixed vocabulary of size $V$, as in autocomplete.
Tokens are words and punctuation marks. At each position $t$, the
{context} is the text so far in the paragraph,
$w_1, \dots, w_{t-1}$, and the {truth} is the token that actually
comes next, $w_t$. The {prediction} is a probability distribution
over the vocabulary, $P(w_t \mid w_1, \dots, w_{t-1})$, and its $k$ most
probable tokens are offered as suggestions for the next word.

A good model gives high probability to the true next token and ranks it
among its top suggestions. The main difficulty is that most contexts are
rare: many word sequences appear only a few times in the training text,
or never, so the model must generalize from similar contexts it has seen.

\paragraph{Demo.} A small demo (\texttt{src/predict\_demo.py}) shows the
task in practice. The user types the beginning of a sentence, and each
selected model suggests the five most likely next words (the number can
be changed). \texttt{<unk>} and \texttt{<eos>} are never offered as suggestions, and punctuation can optionally be hidden. The demo runs on the trained models saved in the
repository, so no retraining is needed. The repository's
\texttt{README.md} explains how to run the demo and how to reproduce all
experiments.

\section{Data}
\label{sec:data}

\subsection{Source corpus}

The corpus is the complete Sherlock Holmes canon by Arthur Conan Doyle: nine public-domain works obtained from Project Gutenberg~\cite{doyle}, consisting of four novels and five short-story anthologies.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Novels: {A Study in Scarlet}, {The Sign of Four},
    {The Hound of the Baskervilles}, {The Valley of Fear}.
    \item Anthologies: {The Adventures of Sherlock Holmes},
    {The Memoirs of Sherlock Holmes}, {The Return of Sherlock
    Holmes}, {His Last Bow}, {The Case-Book of Sherlock Holmes}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Preprocessing pipeline}

Preprocessing (\texttt{src/preprocess.py}, \texttt{src/data\_utils.py})
follows the WikiText benchmark conventions~\cite{merity2016}, which use
the Moses tokenizer~\cite{koehn2007}:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Boilerplate removal.} Project Gutenberg license headers
    and footers are stripped.

    \item \textbf{Unit segmentation.} Anthologies are split into stories
    by locating each title from the ``Contents'' listing in the body text;
    novels stay whole. This gives 60 units: 56 stories and 4 novels.

    \item \textbf{Paragraphs.} Each sequence is one paragraph (text
    between blank lines), ending with \texttt{<eos>}; there is no sentence
    splitting.

    \item \textbf{Heading removal.} Titles, chapter headings, contents
    listings and bylines are dropped (WikiText keeps section titles; here
    they are layout, not content).

    \item \textbf{Normalization.} Variants of the same symbol are unified
    (e.g., curly/straight quotes).

    \item \textbf{Tokenization.} Case is kept, every punctuation mark is a
    token, and numbers are kept. Clitics are split (\texttt{don 't},
    \texttt{Holmes 's}), inner hyphens and number separators become markers
    (\texttt{well @-@ known}, \texttt{1 @,@ 000}), and title abbreviations
    stay whole (\texttt{Mr.}, \texttt{Dr.}).

    \item \textbf{Leak-free splitting.} Whole units are assigned to splits,
    so no story appears in two splits. Units are shuffled and
    filled into test, validation, then train, until each reaches its token
    target (10\%, 10\%, rest).

    \item \textbf{Vocabulary.} Built from the training split only: every
    token seen at least twice (WikiText uses three; this corpus is smaller so we used two), plus \texttt{<unk>} and
    \texttt{<eos>}.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Models}
% ============================================================

All four models are trained and evaluated on the same encoded
paragraphs, each ending in \texttt{<eos>} and each processed on its own
(no model sees context from a previous paragraph), and share the same
vocabulary, so every model predicts the same tokens and their outputs are directly comparable.

\subsection{$n$-gram model with hierarchical Dirichlet smoothing}

The simplest model tested is the $n$-gram model, which predicts the next word from the $n-1$ words before it,
using counts from the training data. Raw counts give probability zero to
any word never seen after a context, so we smooth them with the
hierarchical Dirichlet scheme of MacKay and Peto~\cite{mackay1995}. Let
$h_k$ be the last $k$ words of the context and
$c(h_k, w) = \sum_t \mathbf{1}\big[(w_{t-k}, \dots, w_{t-1}) = h_k,\ w_t = w\big]$
the number of times $w$ followed $h_k$ in training, with
$c(h_k) = \sum_w c(h_k, w)$. Then
\[
P_k(w \mid h_k) = \frac{c(h_k, w) + \alpha_k\, P_{k-1}(w \mid h_{k-1})}
{c(h_k) + \alpha_k}, \qquad P_{-1}(w) = \frac{1}{V},
\]
so the trigram estimate is pulled toward the bigram, the bigram toward the
unigram, and the unigram toward uniform. This is the posterior mean (and,
by conjugacy, the posterior predictive) under a prior
$\theta_{h_k} \sim \text{Dirichlet}\big(\alpha_k\, P_{k-1}(\cdot \mid
h_{k-1})\big)$ on each context's next-word distribution, with one
concentration $\alpha_k$ per context length $k$.

Here $\alpha_k$ is the prior's total pseudo-count mass: a context's own
counts get weight $c(h_k)/(c(h_k) + \alpha_k)$, so frequent contexts follow
their data while rare or unseen ones fall back on the shorter context.
The concentrations may be shared ($\alpha_k = \alpha$ for every $k$) or
set separately per level.
Plain additive smoothing, in contrast, pulls every context straight
toward the uniform distribution, so a rare trigram context gives an
almost uniform, uninformative prediction instead of falling back on
what the bigram knows about it.

The model trains in a single counting pass and is fully interpretable,
but it sees only the last $n-1$ words and cannot generalise across
similar contexts. Its hyperparameters are the order $n$ and the
concentrations $\alpha_k$. Implemented in \texttt{src/ngram.py}.

\subsection{Hidden Markov Model (point estimate)}

Instead of a fixed window of $n-1$ words, an HMM\cite{rabiner1989} carries the whole
history as a belief over $K$ hidden states. Each word $w_t$ is emitted by
a hidden state $z_t$, with initial distribution $\pi$, transitions
$A_{ij} = P(z_{t+1} = j \mid z_t = i)$ and emissions
$B_{jw} = P(w_t = w \mid z_t = j)$.

The states are unobserved, so we track
$\alpha_t(j) = P(z_t = j \mid w_{1:t})$ with the forward algorithm:
\[
\alpha_t(j) = \frac{1}{c_t}\, B_{j w_t} \sum_i \alpha_{t-1}(i) A_{ij},
\qquad c_t = P(w_t \mid w_{1:t-1}),
\]
starting from $\alpha_1(j) \propto \pi_j B_{j w_1}$. The normaliser $c_t$
prevents underflow and gives $\log P(w_{1:T}) = \sum_t \log c_t$. To
predict the next word we move the belief one step forward and emit:
$P(w_{t+1} = w \mid w_{1:t}) = \sum_{i,j} \alpha_t(i) A_{ij} B_{jw}$.

We train with Baum-Welch (EM). The E-step uses forward-backward to get
the expected state and transition counts,
$\gamma_t(i) = P(z_t = i \mid w_{1:T})$ and
$\xi_t(i,j) = P(z_t = i, z_{t+1} = j \mid w_{1:T})$. The M-step normalises
these counts, with a small additive smoothing term $\epsilon$. For the transitions,
\(
A_{ij} = \frac{\sum_t \xi_t(i,j) + \epsilon}{\sum_{t<T} \gamma_t(i) + K\epsilon},
\)
and analogously for $B$ and $\pi$. The smoothing prevents zero
probabilities for transitions or emissions that receive zero expected
counts. The resulting model is a point estimate of $\pi$, $A$, and $B$,
with no posterior uncertainty over these parameters, which the VB-HMM
below addresses.
Parameters are randomly initialised to break symmetry between the
hidden states, and EM finds only a local optimum. 
The main hyperparameter is the number of hidden states $K$. Implemented in \texttt{src/hmm.py}.

\subsection{Variational Bayes HMM (full posterior)}

The HMM above keeps a single value for each parameter, so it cannot tell
a transition seen once from one seen a thousand times. The VB-HMM keeps
the same model but puts Dirichlet priors on the parameters,
$A_{i\cdot} \sim \text{Dir}(\alpha_0)$, $B_{j\cdot} \sim \text{Dir}(\beta_0)$,
$\pi \sim \text{Dir}(\pi_0)$, and fits an approximate posterior
$q(z)\,q(\pi, A, B)$ by mean-field Variational Bayes~\cite{beal2003,bishop2006}.

By conjugacy, the posterior stays Dirichlet, so the M-step just adds the
expected counts to the prior:
$\hat\alpha_{ij} = \alpha_0 + \sum_t \xi_t(i,j)$. The E-step is the same
forward-backward, but with each parameter replaced by
\[
\widetilde{A}_{ij} = \exp\big(\mathbb{E}_q[\log A_{ij}]\big)
= \exp\Big(\psi(\hat\alpha_{ij}) - \psi\big(\textstyle\sum_k \hat\alpha_{ik}\big)\Big),
\]
where $\psi$ is the digamma function. For large counts this is close to
$\hat\alpha_{ij} / \sum_k \hat\alpha_{ik}$, but for small counts it is
smaller, so rare transitions,
emissions and states are down-weighted automatically. Training maximises
the ELBO, $\sum_t \log \widetilde{c}_t - \mathrm{KL}(q \,\|\, p)$, which
never decreases.
For prediction we use the posterior mean
$\hat\alpha_{ij} / \sum_k \hat\alpha_{ik}$, so the model plugs into the
same forward filter as the HMM. 
Implemented in \texttt{src/vb\_hmm.py}.


\subsection{LSTM recurrent neural network}

Like the HMM, the LSTM~\cite{hochreiter1997} carries the history in a
state updated one word at a time, but the state is a learned continuous
vector rather than a belief over discrete states. Each token is mapped to
a learned embedding $e_t$, and the LSTM keeps a hidden state $h_t$ and a
memory cell $c_t$, controlled by forget, input and output gates:
\[
\begin{aligned}
f_t, i_t, o_t &= \sigma\big(W_{f,i,o}\, e_t + U_{f,i,o}\, h_{t-1} + b_{f,i,o}\big),
\qquad g_t = \tanh(W_g e_t + U_g h_{t-1} + b_g), \\
c_t &= f_t \odot c_{t-1} + i_t \odot g_t, \qquad h_t = o_t \odot \tanh(c_t).
\end{aligned}
\]
The forget gate decides how much of the old memory to keep, while the
input gate decides how much of the candidate content $g_t$ to write to
the cell. Because $c_t$ is updated
additively, information and gradients can propagate across many time steps, which helps with the
vanishing-gradient problem of plain RNNs. With two layers, the second LSTM layer takes the first layer's hidden state at each time step as input. A linear layer maps the top $h_t$ to the next-word
distribution,
\[
P_\theta(w_{t+1} = w \mid w_{1:t}) = \mathrm{softmax}(W_{\text{out}} h_t + b_{\text{out}})_w ,
\]
where $\theta$ denotes all model parameters.

Dropout is applied to the embeddings and to the LSTM outputs (and between
layers when there are two). Similar contexts can lead to similar hidden
states, allowing information learned from one context to generalize to
others rather than relying only on exact count matches.

Like the other models, the LSTM processes each paragraph on
its own: the hidden state starts at zero and the first input is
\texttt{<eos>} (the paragraph-start context), so every token of the
paragraph, including the first, is predicted, and no context crosses
paragraph boundaries. 
Training uses mini-batches of paragraphs of similar length, padded at the end. On each mini-batch we minimise the
cross-entropy loss, which, with a single true next token, is its average
negative log-probability,
\[
\mathcal{L}(\theta) = -\frac{1}{\sum_b T_b}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\sum_{t=1}^{T_b}
\log P_\theta\big(w^{(b)}_{t} \mid w^{(b)}_{< t}\big),
\]
where $B$ is the number of paragraphs in the mini-batch, $T_b$ is the length of paragraph $b$ (padding is excluded) and
$w^{(b)}_{< t}$ are its preceding tokens, and take one Adam step, with gradient-norm clipping.
Paragraphs are short (median 29 tokens,
at most 710), so gradients are propagated back
through the whole paragraph. Training stops early when validation perplexity stops
improving, and the weights of the best epoch are kept. The result is a
single point estimate $\hat\theta$ of the weights.
The hyperparameters are the embedding size, the hidden size, the number
of layers, the dropout rate, and the learning rate. Implemented in \texttt{src/rnn.py}.

\section{Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:eval}
% ============================================================

All models are scored by the same code (\texttt{src/evaluation.py}) on
exactly the same token positions, so their numbers can be compared
directly. 

\paragraph{Perplexity.} At each position $t$ the model outputs a
predicted distribution $\hat y_t$ over the vocabulary, with
$\hat y_{t,w} = P(w \mid w_{<t})$, and the truth is a one-hot vector
$y_t$ with $y_{t,w} = 1$ only for the true token $w_t$. The average
cross-entropy over all $T$ tokens is
\[
\mathrm{CE} = -\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} \sum_{w \in \mathcal{V}}
y_{t,w} \log \hat y_{t,w}
= -\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} \log P(w_t \mid w_{<t}) = \mathrm{NLL},
\]
since only the true token's term is nonzero. This is the negative
log-likelihood (NLL) of the text, the loss minimised by the LSTM and
plotted as the loss per token in the learning curves. Perplexity is its
exponential, $\mathrm{PPL} = \exp(\mathrm{NLL})$.
Perplexity can be read as the number of words the model is effectively
choosing between at each step: a model that guesses uniformly over the
vocabulary has perplexity $V$, and lower is better. Unlike accuracy, it
uses the full predicted distribution, so it rewards a model for giving
the true word high probability even when it is not ranked first. All
tokens are scored, including the first token of each paragraph, which is
predicted from a start distribution (the HMM's initial-state
distribution; for the $n$-gram and the LSTM, the distribution after the
paragraph-start token).

\paragraph{Top-$k$ accuracy.} For $k \in \{1, 5\}$, the fraction of
positions where the true next token is among the $k$ most probable
predictions. The first token of each paragraph is not scored, since it is
predicted with no context at all. Punctuation is easy to predict and
inflates accuracy, so accuracy is reported both over all tokens and over
real words only (excluding punctuation and \texttt{<eos>}).

\texttt{<unk>} is treated as an ordinary token, as in
WikiText~\cite{merity2016}. Since all models share the same vocabulary,
this affects them all equally. Every model processes each paragraph
independently, starting from its initial state, so no model can use text
from earlier paragraphs.

\paragraph{Validation and test.} The validation split is used for all
choices: hyperparameters are selected by validation perplexity, and the
LSTM's early stopping also uses it. The test split is used only once, at
the end, to compare the best configuration of each model family
(Section~\ref{sec:test}).

\section{Experimental Design}
\label{sec:design}
% ============================================================

Every configuration is trained on the training split
(\texttt{src/experiment.py}) and compared by validation perplexity.

\paragraph{$n$-gram.} Order $n \in \{2, 3\}$, first with a single shared
concentration $\alpha \in \{10, 30, 100, 300\}$. Because $\alpha_k$ is a
total pseudo-count rather than a per-word one, values of order 1 or below
would make a context seen only once put most of its mass on the single
word that followed it; values in the tens to hundreds let rare contexts
rely mostly on the shorter context while frequent contexts keep their
own counts. We then tested separate concentrations per level. Longer
contexts are rarer, so the sparse trigram level might need more smoothing
than the better-estimated bigram level, which a shared $\alpha$ cannot
provide. The levels are tuned in stages: the bigram's best value
($\alpha = 100$) is kept for the lower levels, and only the trigram
level's $\alpha_2$ is searched over $\{30, 100, 300\}$.

\paragraph{HMM and VB-HMM.} $K \in \{4, 8, 16, 32, 64\}$ hidden states,
the same grid for both. The VB-HMM uses uniform priors on transitions and
initial state ($\alpha_0 = \pi_0 = 1$) and a sparse emission prior
($\beta_0 = 0.1$). At the best $K$ ($K = 64$), the emission prior is
also varied, $\beta_0 \in \{0.01, 0.1, 1\}$.

\paragraph{LSTM.} In two steps. First, embedding size, dropout, and
learning rate are tuned with Optuna's TPE sampler~\cite{akiba2019}
(\texttt{src/tune\_rnn.py}) at a fixed hidden size of 128 and one layer:
50 trials of up to 40 epochs each, with early stopping on the validation perplexity, each
scored by its best validation perplexity. The best configuration is then retrained for up
to 50 epochs. Second, with those three values
fixed, we train every combination of hidden size $\{64, 128, 256\}$ and
$\{1, 2\}$ layers, each with early stopping on validation perplexity.

\paragraph{Training settings.} The following settings are not tuned and
are the same in every run. The HMM uses smoothing $\epsilon = 10^{-3}$
in the M-step. The HMM and VB-HMM stop when the relative change of their
training objective (the log-likelihood and the ELBO, respectively) is
below $10^{-4}$ for 3 consecutive iterations, or after at most 200
iterations. The LSTM uses mini-batches of 20 paragraphs, Adam with the
gradient norm clipped at $0.25$, and early stopping with a patience of 5
epochs.

\paragraph{$n$-gram.} For both orders, perplexity decreases as $\alpha$
grows up to $\alpha = 100$ and rises again at $\alpha = 300$, where the
model smooths too much and relies too heavily on the shorter context
(Figure~\ref{fig:sweeps}(a)). With too little smoothing ($\alpha = 10$)
the trigram is worse than the bigram, likely because it trusts rarely
seen contexts too much; with enough smoothing it is clearly better.
Since trigram contexts are much sparser than bigram contexts, the
trigram level might need a different amount of smoothing than the lower
levels, which a single shared $\alpha$ cannot provide. We therefore also
tried a separate concentration for the trigram level, keeping
$\alpha = 100$ for the lower levels. Both less smoothing
($\alpha_2 = 30$, perplexity 121.1) and more smoothing
($\alpha_2 = 300$, perplexity 116.2) gave a higher perplexity than the
shared $\alpha = 100$ (115.9). 
Less smoothing gives slightly higher top-1 accuracy, but we select on
perplexity.


\paragraph{HMM and VB-HMM.} Both models improve steadily with $K$ and are still improving at $K = 64$ (Figure~\ref{fig:sweeps}(b)): we stopped there because the cost of EM grows as
$K^2$. The VB-HMM is worse than the HMM at every $K$.
For its emission prior, a
uniform prior ($\beta_0 = 1$) is much worse, and $\beta_0 = 0.01$ is
about the same as $\beta_0 = 0.1$.

The loss curves (Figure~\ref{fig:curves}) suggest that the VB-HMM
underfits. At $K=64$, its training loss remains substantially higher
than the HMM's (about 5.2 versus 4.7 nats per token). At the same time,
the HMM's validation loss continues to decrease, suggesting that
overfitting is not the dominant issue. In this setting, the Bayesian
regularization appears to reduce model flexibility more than it improves
generalization.
Two aspects of the VB-HMM likely contribute to this behavior. First, the
Dirichlet emission prior adds $V\beta_0 \approx 1{,}100$ pseudo-counts to each state's
emission distribution,
effectively allocating a non-negligible fraction of the probability mass
to a uniform background distribution rather than to words frequently
emitted by that state. This also explains why $\beta_0=1$ performs much
worse. Second, the $\exp(\mathbb{E}_q[\log B])$ terms used in the VB
E-step are approximately equivalent to discounting each count by one half~\cite{johnson2007,asuncion2009}, which suppresses
rare words and rarely used states.

The VB-HMM's validation loss is even slightly lower than its training
loss(Figure~\ref{fig:curves}(b)). A likely reason is that the validation text is easier for
low-capacity models: it has almost three times as many \texttt{<unk>}
tokens (3.1\% vs.\ 1.2\%), and \texttt{<unk>}, which merges all rare
words, is easier to predict than an average word. The HMM with $K = 4$
shows the same effect.


\paragraph{LSTM.} The Optuna search (50 trials, hidden size 128, one
layer) selected an embedding size of 256, dropout 0.29 and learning
rate $6.2 \cdot 10^{-4}$, with a validation perplexity of 78.0. These
values are used for all LSTMs below. Hidden size matters most: going
from hidden size 64 to 256 lowers perplexity from about 87 to 73, while a
second layer changes it by less than one point
(Figure~\ref{fig:sweeps}(c)). Larger networks start to overfit sooner,
and early stopping keeps the best epoch (Figure~\ref{fig:curves}).


\paragraph{Comparison.} The LSTM is clearly best (72.9), followed by the trigram (115.9), the HMM (132.9) and the VB-HMM (172.9). The $n$-gram is a strong baseline here: its hierarchical smoothing lets a rare trigram
context fall back on the bigram and then the unigram, instead of
guessing, and its concentration $\alpha$ was tuned. The HMM, in
contrast, predicts the next word only through its hidden state, so all
information about the context must pass through a belief over 64
states, while the $n$-gram keeps a separate distribution for every
context seen in training. The HMM was also explored less: only up to
$K = 64$, where it is still improving, from a single random
initialisation, since EM finds only a local optimum. With more states
it would likely come closer; it is already close to the bigram
(132.9 vs.\ 128.6). 
The ranking is the same for top-$k$ accuracy, but
the gap between the LSTM and the trigram is much smaller: 46.4\% vs.\
43.1\% top-5 accuracy, compared with 72.9 vs.\ 115.9 in perplexity. The
trigram often ranks the true word among its top suggestions, but gives
it lower probability than the LSTM does.
